Newtonian Dynamics & The Physical Definition of Force

Force is an essential vector physical quantity that quantifies the interaction capable of changing an object’s velocity, causing linear acceleration or rotational angular acceleration, or creating mechanical strain and deformation within a physical body. Formulated by Sir Isaac Newton in his 1687 masterwork Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, classical dynamics defines net force through Newton's Second Law of Motion as the time rate of change of linear momentum (\(\vec{p} = m \cdot \vec{v}\)):

$$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = \frac{d(m\vec{v})}{dt} = m \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} + \vec{v} \frac{dm}{dt}$$

For systems with constant mass (\(dm/dt = 0\)), this simplifies to the foundational scalar relationship:

$$F = m \cdot a$$

In dimensional analysis, force possesses the fundamental dimensions of mass multiplied by length divided by time squared:

$$\text{dim}(F) = [M][L][T^{-2}]$$

The coherent SI derived unit of force is the Newton (\(\text{N}\)). One Newton represents the exact force required to impart an acceleration of one meter per second squared (\(1\text{ m/s}^2\)) to a mass of one kilogram (\(1\text{ kg}\)):

$$1 \text{ N} = 1 \frac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 10^5 \text{ dynes (exact)}$$

In structural engineering, geotechnical foundation design, and aerospace thrust modeling, decimal multiples dominate: the kilonewton (\(\text{kN} = 10^3\text{ N}\)) represents typical column and bolt shear loads, while the meganewton (\(\text{MN} = 10^6\text{ N}\)) quantifies rocket engine thrust (e.g., the Saturn V rocket first stage generated approximately \(35.1\text{ MN}\) of liftoff thrust).

Gravitational Force Systems vs. Absolute Inertial Systems

A primary source of historical friction between engineering traditions lies in the division between absolute force systems and gravitational force systems:

  1. Absolute Systems (SI & CGS): The base unit of mass (the kilogram or gram) is independent of local gravity. The unit of force (the Newton or dyne) is derived dynamically from acceleration: \(F = m \cdot a\).
  2. Gravitational Systems (US Customary & Metric Technical): The base unit was historically chosen as the force exerted by Earth’s gravity on a standard mass at sea level. This led to the pound-force (\(\text{lbf}\)) in the US and the kilogram-force (\(\text{kgf}\) or kilopond \(\text{kp}\)) in continental Europe.

To establish rigorous international definitions, the 1901 3rd CGPM standardized international standard gravity as an exact constant:

$$g_0 = 9.80665 \text{ m/s}^2 \approx 32.1740485564 \text{ ft/s}^2 \text{ (exact)}$$

From this invariant gravitational baseline, all gravitational force units are defined mathematically:

1. The Pound-Force (lbf)

Under NIST SP 811, one pound-force is the downward gravitational pull exerted on one international avoirdupois pound (\(0.45359237\text{ kg}\)) in standard gravity:

$$1 \text{ lbf} = 0.45359237 \text{ kg} \times 9.80665 \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 4.4482216152605 \text{ Newtons (exact)}$$ $$1 \text{ N} = \frac{1}{4.4482216152605} \text{ lbf} \approx 0.2248089430997 \text{ lbf}$$

2. The Kilogram-Force (kgf / kp)

Also known historically as the kilopond in Germany and Scandinavia, one kilogram-force represents standard gravity acting on a 1-kg mass:

$$1 \text{ kgf} = 1 \text{ kp} = 1.0 \text{ kg} \times 9.80665 \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 9.80665 \text{ Newtons (exact)}$$

3. The Dyne (dyn)

The base unit of force in the centimeter-gram-second (CGS) system, one dyne accelerates 1 gram at \(1\text{ cm/s}^2\):

$$1 \text{ dyn} = 1 \frac{\text{g}\cdot\text{cm}}{\text{s}^2} = 10^{-3} \text{ kg} \times 10^{-2} \text{ m/s}^2 = 10^{-5} \text{ N} = 10 \text{ µN (exact)}$$

Dynes remain widely employed in fluid mechanics to measure liquid surface tension (e.g., water at \(20^\circ\text{C}\) has a surface tension of \(72.8\text{ dyn/cm} = 72.8\text{ mN/m}\)).

American Structural Engineering: The Kip Standard

In United States civil and structural engineering (governed by the American Institute of Steel Construction, AISC, and the American Concrete Institute, ACI), calculating structural loads using individual pounds-force results in unwieldy seven-figure numbers. A high-rise steel column carrying \(1{,}450{,}000\text{ pounds}\) is awkward to write on engineering blueprints and prone to drafting clerical errors.

To streamline structural calculations, American engineers adopted the kip (a portmanteau of "kilo-pound"):

$$1 \text{ kip} = 1{,}000 \text{ lbf} = 4{,}448.221615 \text{ Newtons} \approx 4.448222 \text{ kN}$$

From the kip, two primary structural units are derived:

  • Kips per Square Inch (ksi): Standard unit for material tensile yield strength and modulus of elasticity (\(1\text{ ksi} = 1{,}000\text{ psi} \approx 6.894757\text{ MPa}\)). For example, ASTM A36 structural steel has a minimum yield strength of \(F_y = 36\text{ ksi} \approx 248.2\text{ MPa}\).
  • Foot-Kips (ft·kip): Standard unit for bending moments and beam torque (\(1\text{ ft}\cdot\text{kip} = 1{,}000\text{ ft}\cdot\text{lbf} \approx 1{,}355.818\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\)).

Worked Civil Structural Example: Highway Overpass Pier Bearing Pad Reaction

Bridge Engineering Design Scenario

A structural bridge engineer is analyzing a prestressed concrete highway overpass carrying interstate traffic. Finite element structural analysis yields a factored dead load plus live load vertical downward reaction at an elastomeric pier bearing pad of:

$$R_{\text{pier}} = 420.0 \text{ kips}$$

The state Department of Transportation (DOT) specification requires elastomeric bearing pads sourced from European manufacturers certified under Eurocode 1337-3. The European manufacturer’s technical data sheets specify maximum design vertical compressive capacity in kilonewtons (kN) and metric tonnes-force (tf). The project engineer must convert the pier reaction force across both metric units.

Step 1: Convert kips to pounds-force

$$R_{\text{lbf}} = 420.0 \text{ kips} \times 1{,}000 \frac{\text{lbf}}{\text{kip}} = 420{,}000 \text{ lbf}$$

Step 2: Convert pounds-force to Newtons using exact NIST multipliers

$$R_{\text{N}} = 420{,}000 \text{ lbf} \times 4.448221615 \frac{\text{N}}{\text{lbf}} = 1{,}868{,}253.08 \text{ Newtons}$$

Step 3: Convert Newtons to Kilonewtons

$$R_{\text{kN}} = \frac{1{,}868{,}253.08 \text{ N}}{1{,}000 \text{ N/kN}} = 1{,}868.25 \text{ kN}$$

Step 4: Convert Newtons to Metric Tonnes-Force

Using the exact relation \(1\text{ tf} = 9{,}806.65\text{ N}\):

$$R_{\text{tf}} = \frac{1{,}868{,}253.08 \text{ N}}{9{,}806.65 \text{ N/tf}} \approx 190.51 \text{ metric tonnes-force}$$

Conclusion: The overpass bearing reaction is 1,868.25 kN (190.51 tonnes-force). The engineer must select an elastomeric bearing pad with a certified Eurocode compressive capacity rating of at least \(2{,}000\text{ kN}\) to ensure safe structural performance.

Precision Metrology: Avoiding Mass-Force Conflation in Structural Modeling

A frequent error in dynamic finite element analysis (FEA) occurs when engineers conflate mass with force. In dynamic seismic analysis (earthquake response spectrum modeling), structural mass (\(m = W / g\)) must be supplied in true mass units (kilograms or slugs), while seismic base shear forces are computed in kilonewtons or kips. Inputting weight in kilograms-force as if it were inertial mass without dividing by gravitational acceleration (\(g_0\)) causes the software to miscalculate structural natural frequencies by a factor of \(9.81\).
