💡 Direct Answer (GEO & Quick Summary)

Maximum beam deflection is governed by classical Euler-Bernoulli beam theory:

For a simply supported beam under uniformly distributed load (UDL): δmax = (5 × w × L^4) ÷ (384 × E × I)

For a center concentrated point load: δmax = (P × L^3) ÷ (48 × E × I)

Where w is load per unit length, P is point load, L is span length, E is material Young's modulus, and I is the cross-sectional second moment of area. Under AISC 360 and Eurocode 3, live load deflection is limited to L / 360 for plaster ceilings and L / 250 for general serviceability.

Euler-Bernoulli Beam Bending Differential Equations

In structural analysis and mechanical design, beams are structural members subjected to transverse lateral loads that induce internal bending moments, shear stresses, and physical curvature. The foundational mathematical model governing beam bending is the Euler-Bernoulli Beam Theory (also designated classical beam theory), which operates on the kinematic hypothesis that plane cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the neutral longitudinal axis during flexural deformation.

The fundamental differential equation relating applied transverse load $q(x)$, internal shear force $V(x)$, bending moment $M(x)$, slope $\theta(x)$, and transverse deflection $v(x)$ is formulated as:

\[ E I \frac{d^4 v}{dx^4} = q(x), \quad E I \frac{d^3 v}{dx^3} = -V(x), \quad E I \frac{d^2 v}{dx^2} = -M(x), \quad \frac{dv}{dx} = \theta(x) \]

Where the product $E I$ represents the flexural rigidity of the beam member — combining the intrinsic material stiffness (Young's modulus $E$) with the cross-sectional geometric distribution of material away from the neutral axis (second moment of area $I$). Integrating these boundary conditions across standard support configurations yields exact closed-form algebraic solutions for maximum deflection.

Standard Deflection & Moment Closed-Form Formulas

The classical closed-form equations for maximum deflection ($\delta_{\max}$), maximum bending moment ($M_{\max}$), and shear forces across standard load conditions:

1. Simply Supported Beam with Uniformly Distributed Load (UDL)
\delta_{\max} = \frac{5 w L^4}{384 E I} \quad (\text{at center } x = L/2), \quad M_{\max} = \frac{w L^2}{8}, \quad V_{\max} = \frac{w L}{2}
Where $w$ is uniform load per unit length (kN/m), $L$ is clear span, $E$ is elastic modulus, and $I$ is moment of inertia.
2. Simply Supported Beam with Concentrated Point Load at Center
\delta_{\max} = \frac{P L^3}{48 E I} \quad (\text{at center } x = L/2), \quad M_{\max} = \frac{P L}{4}, \quad V_{\max} = \frac{P}{2}
Where $P$ is concentrated point force (kN) applied precisely at midspan.
3. Cantilever Beam (Fixed-Free) with Uniformly Distributed Load
\delta_{\max} = \frac{w L^4}{8 E I} \quad (\text{at free end } x = L), \quad M_{\max} = \frac{w L^2}{2} \quad (\text{at fixed support}), \quad V_{\max} = w L
Cantilever deflects 9.6 times greater than an equivalent simply supported beam under identical uniform loading!
4. Cantilever Beam (Fixed-Free) with Point Load at Free End
\delta_{\max} = \frac{P L^3}{3 E I} \quad (\text{at free tip } x = L), \quad M_{\max} = P L \quad (\text{at fixed root}), \quad V_{\max} = P
Produces 16 times greater tip deflection than a simply supported beam with center load.

Serviceability Limit State (SLS) Deflection Limits

In modern Limit State Design (LSD / LRFD), structural sizing must satisfy two distinct independent criteria:

  • Ultimate Limit State (ULS): Structural strength safety against catastrophic plastic yielding, shear rupture, and lateral-torsional buckling ($M_u \le \phi M_n$).
  • Serviceability Limit State (SLS): Controlling operational deformations under unfactored live and dead service loads to prevent aesthetic sagging, cracking of attached drywall/plaster ceilings, ponding of rainwater on roofs, and psychological discomfort of building occupants caused by perceptible floor bounce.

International building codes (IBC Table 1604.3 and Eurocode EN 1990) establish clear span-to-deflection ratios ($\delta_{\text{allow}} = L / \kappa$):

  • $L / 360$: The classic live-load deflection limit for floor beams supporting plaster ceilings or brittle finishes that crack under minor differential sag.
  • $L / 240$: Total load deflection limit (Dead + Live load) for commercial floor beams and primary roof framing.
  • $L / 180$: Non-plastered roof purlins, industrial crane runway beams, and cantilever overhangs where visual appearance is secondary.
  • $L / 480$: Sensitive architectural conditions, such as beams directly supporting heavy masonry veneer walls or structural glass curtain wall assemblies.

📐 Comprehensive Worked Case Study: W16x40 Structural Steel Floor Girder Analysis

Structural Steel Design Case Study
Step 1: Define Span, Section Geometry & Applied Service Loads
\[ L = 7.50\text{ m},\quad w_{\text{service}} = 18.0\text{ kN/m},\quad E = 200\text{ GPa},\quad I_x = 216 \times 10^6\text{ mm}^4 = 2.16 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^4 \]

A W16 × 40 structural steel floor girder spans 7.5 meters between columns, supporting a total unfactored service UDL of 18 kN/m.

Step 2: Calculate Flexural Rigidity (EI) & Maximum Bending Moment
\[ E I = (200 \times 10^6\text{ kN/m}^2) \times (2.16 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^4) = 43{,}200\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}^2 \] \[ M_{\max} = \frac{w L^2}{8} = \frac{18.0 \times 7.50^2}{8} = 126.56\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m},\quad V_{\max} = \frac{18.0 \times 7.50}{2} = 67.50\text{ kN} \]

Bending moment at midspan equals 126.56 kN·m; end shear reactions are 67.5 kN.

Step 3: Compute Midspan Maximum Elastic Deflection
\[ \delta_{\max} = \frac{5 w L^4}{384 E I} = \frac{5 \times 18.0 \times (7.50)^4}{384 \times 43{,}200} = \frac{284{,}765.6}{16{,}588{,}800} = 0.01717\text{ m} = 17.17\text{ mm} \]

Maximum elastic sagging deflection at midspan equals 17.17 mm.

Step 4: Check Serviceability Limit State Against L/360 Criteria
\[ \delta_{\text{allowable}} = \frac{L}{360} = \frac{7{,}500\text{ mm}}{360} = 20.83\text{ mm} \] \[ \delta_{\max} = 17.17\text{ mm} \le 20.83\text{ mm} \quad (\text{Utilization Ratio: } 82.4\% \implies \textbf{PASS}) \]

Actual span-to-deflection ratio is $L / 437$, satisfying the strict $L / 360$ architectural limit without pre-cambering.

Frequently Asked Questions (Beam Deflection & Flexure)

Why does beam deflection increase with the fourth power of span length (L^4)?
For distributed loads, increasing span length creates a compound compounding effect: the total load on the beam increases linearly with length ($W = w \cdot L$), the moment arm of that load increases linearly with length (yielding $M \propto L^2$), and the flexural curvature integrates across the longer span twice to determine deflection. Mathematically: $\delta \propto w \cdot L^4 / (E I)$. Consequently, doubling the clear span of a floor girder without changing its section size increases elastic deflection by a staggering 16 times ($2^4 = 16$)!
What is the difference between moment of inertia (I) and section modulus (S)?
The Second Moment of Area / Inertia ($I$) has dimensions of length to the fourth power ($mm^4$ or $in^4$) and governs deflection and stiffness: $\delta \propto 1 / (E I)$. The Elastic Section Modulus ($S = I / y$) has dimensions of length cubed ($mm^3$ or $in^3$) and governs bending stress and strength: $\sigma = M / S$. While section modulus determines whether a beam will rupture or yield, moment of inertia determines how far it will physically sag under service load.
How does concrete cracking affect long-term beam deflection in ACI 318?
Unlike homogeneous structural steel, reinforced concrete cracks under tensile flexure once tensile stress exceeds the modulus of rupture ($f_r$). Under ACI 318-19 Section 24.2, structural engineers must calculate the Effective Moment of Inertia ($I_e$) using Branson's equation, interpolating between the uncracked gross inertia ($I_g$) and the cracked section inertia ($I_{cr}$). Furthermore, sustained long-term dead loads cause concrete creep and shrinkage, which multiplies immediate dead load deflection by a time-dependent factor $\lambda_{\Delta} = \xi / (1 + 50 \rho')$, frequently doubling or tripling deflection over 5 years.
What is beam pre-cambering and when is it recommended?
Pre-cambering is the intentional manufacturing of a slight upward curvature (typically using hydraulic jacks or heat induction) into a steel or glulam beam prior to installation. The magnitude of pre-camber is designed to exactly counteract expected permanent dead load deflection. When concrete floor slabs and permanent architectural finishes are placed, the beam deflects downward to a perfectly flat, level plane, ensuring smooth floors and eliminating visual sagging.
