Engineering Guide to Standby & Prime Generator Sizing

Properly sizing an on-site diesel or natural gas generator set (genset) requires an engineering balance between engine mechanical power (measured in brake horsepower or kWm) and alternator electromagnetic capacity (measured in kVA). A generator set that is undersized will suffer engine stalling under sudden load applications, unmanageable transient voltage dips that trip sensitive electronics, or continuous overheating. Conversely, a generator that is severely oversized ($> 60\%$ excess capacity) will suffer from wet stacking—a condition where unburned fuel and carbon accumulate in the diesel exhaust system due to low combustion cylinder temperatures, causing valve carbonization, oil contamination, and permanent engine glazing.

Professional genset dimensioning conforms to international standards codified in ISO 8528 (Reciprocating internal combustion engine driven alternating current generating sets), NFPA 110 (Standard for Emergency and Standby Power Systems), and IEEE 446 (Orange Book: Recommended Practice for Emergency and Standby Power Systems).

Steady-State Running Load & Power Factor Conversion

Electrical loads are specified in continuous running kilowatts ($kW$). Because commercial AC generators are wound for an industry-standard $0.80$ lagging power factor ($\text{PF} = \cos\theta = 0.8$), the required alternator apparent power in kilovolt-amperes ($kVA$) is derived from:

$$kVA_{steady} = \frac{kW_{running}}{\text{PF}} = \frac{kW_{running}}{0.80}$$

Where total running load combines linear static loads (lighting, heating elements, office equipment) and motor running power:

$$kW_{running} = kW_{static} + (HP_{motors} \times 0.746\text{ kW/HP}) + kW_{non-linear}$$

Motor Starting Inrush: The SkVA Transient Shock

Electric induction motors represent the most severe transient challenge for an isolated generator. At the instant of starting, a stationary motor acts essentially as a short-circuited transformer secondary, drawing an initial locked-rotor current ($I_{LRA}$) far exceeding its nameplate full-load running current.

Under NEMA MG-1 standards, motor starting kVA ($SkVA$) is categorized by NEMA Design Code Letters (ranging from Code A to Code V). Most modern industrial motors conform to Code G, which exhibits a locked-rotor kVA of approximately $5.6\text{ to }6.3\text{ kVA per rated horsepower}$:

$$SkVA_{base} = HP_{motor} \times 6.0 \quad [\text{kVA}]$$

The actual surge seen by the alternator depends directly upon the motor starting architecture:

Motor Starter Topology Inrush Multiplier Starting Torque (% FLT) Generator Sizing Impact
Direct-on-Line (DOL / Across-the-Line) $100\%$ ($6.0\times$ running HP) $150\% - 200\%$ Requires massive alternator surge headroom; voltage dip typically $25\% - 35\%$
Star-Delta Starter (Wye-Delta) $33\%$ ($2.0\times$ running HP) $33\%$ Substantial reduction in inrush; suitable for low starting-torque loads (centrifugal pumps)
Solid-State Soft Starter $45\% - 55\%$ ($2.8\times - 3.2\times$ running HP) Adjustable ($30\% - 70\%$) Smooth current ramp; limits transient alternator voltage dip to under $15\%$
Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) $20\%$ ($1.0\times - 1.2\times$ running HP) $100\% - 150\%$ Eliminates starting kVA surge entirely; however, introduces harmonic distortion

Transient Voltage Dip Evaluation (ISO 8528-5)

When the starting kVA shock ($SkVA$) is applied across the generator alternator, the instantaneous terminal voltage dips due to the internal subtransient reactance ($X''_d$) of the alternator:

$$\text{Voltage Dip } (\Delta V\%) \approx \frac{SkVA}{kVA_{generator} + SkVA} \times 100\%$$

For critical facilities (data centers, hospitals, industrial automation), ISO 8528-5 Class G2 and G3 standards mandate that transient voltage dip must not exceed $15\%$ to $20\%$. An excessive voltage dip will cause magnetic contactors and relays to drop out (typically opening at $< 65\%$ nominal voltage), causing connected plant equipment to trip offline.

Environmental Derating: Altitude & Ambient Temperature

Combustion engines require ambient air oxygen to combust diesel or natural gas fuel. As site altitude increases, atmospheric air pressure decreases, resulting in lower air density. Concurrently, high ambient switchroom temperatures reduce the volumetric cooling efficiency of both the radiator core and the alternator cooling fan.

Standard rating conditions per ISO 3046 / ISO 8528 are established at sea level ($0\text{ m}$) and $25^\circ\text{C}$ to $40^\circ\text{C}$ ambient. When operating beyond baseline conditions, deratings must be compounded:

  • Altitude Derating ($k_{alt}$): For elevations exceeding $1{,}000\text{ meters}$ ($3{,}280\text{ feet}$), engine power is derated by $3.5\%$ for each additional $300\text{ meters}$ ($1{,}000\text{ feet}$).
  • Temperature Derating ($k_{temp}$): For ambient temperatures exceeding $40^\circ\text{C}$ ($104^\circ\text{F}$), output capacity is derated by $1.0\%$ for each $5^\circ\text{C}$ temperature rise.
  • Combined Environmental Factor: $k_{env} = k_{alt} \times k_{temp}$. The required sea-level commercial generator rating is: $$kVA_{nominal,req} = \frac{kVA_{calculated}}{k_{env}}$$

Practical Worked Engineering Example: Hospital Emergency Generator Sizing

Scenario: Sizing an emergency standby generator (ESP) for a regional hospital healthcare pavilion located at an altitude of $1{,}300\text{ meters}$ where summer switchroom temperatures reach $45^\circ\text{C}$. The connected loads comprise:

  • Continuous static emergency lighting, life-safety, and HVAC: $85\text{ kW}$
  • Largest individual motor: A $30\text{ HP}$ chilled-water pump started Direct-on-Line (DOL)
  • Critical non-linear UPS computing and MRI imaging load: $25\text{ kW}$
  • Design future expansion safety margin: $20\%$

Step 1: Compute Total Running Load in kW and kVA

Total running active power $kW_{run}$:

$$kW_{motor,run} = 30\text{ HP} \times 0.746 = 22.38\text{ kW}$$

$$kW_{total,run} = 85\text{ kW} + 22.38\text{ kW} + 25\text{ kW} = 132.38\text{ kW}$$

At standard $0.80$ power factor:

$$kVA_{running} = \frac{132.38\text{ kW}}{0.80} = 165.48\text{ kVA}$$

Step 2: Account for Non-Linear Harmonic Sizing

Non-linear loads ($25\text{ kW}$) require an additional $25\%$ alternator thermal buffer:

$$kVA_{harmonic,adj} = \frac{25\text{ kW} \times 0.25}{0.80} = 7.81\text{ kVA}$$

Adjusted running demand = $165.48 + 7.81 = 173.29\text{ kVA}$.

Step 3: Evaluate Motor Starting Surge (SkVA) & Voltage Dip

For a $30\text{ HP}$ motor started DOL ($6.0\text{ kVA/HP}$):

$$SkVA = 30 \times 6.0 = 180.0\text{ kVA}$$

To restrict transient voltage dip to $\le 20\%$, the generator must support $SkVA$ within acceptable limits:

$$kVA_{peak,req} \approx \frac{SkVA}{0.20} - SkVA \implies kVA_{min,surge} = \frac{180}{0.20 \times 4} \approx 225\text{ kVA}$$

Step 4: Incorporate 20% Expansion Margin

Base required rating = $\max(173.29\text{ kVA}, 225\text{ kVA}) \times 1.20 = 225 \times 1.20 = 270\text{ kVA}$.

Step 5: Apply Site Altitude and Temperature Deratings

Altitude is $1{,}300\text{ m}$ ($300\text{ m}$ above $1000\text{ m}$ threshold) $\implies k_{alt} = 1.0 - 0.035 = 0.965$.

Temperature is $45^\circ\text{C}$ ($5^\circ\text{C}$ above $40^\circ\text{C}$ threshold) $\implies k_{temp} = 1.0 - 0.010 = 0.990$.

$$k_{env} = 0.965 \times 0.990 = 0.955$$

$$kVA_{final} = \frac{270\text{ kVA}}{0.955} = 282.72\text{ kVA}$$

Commercial Selection: Standard commercial diesel generator sizing increments are 250 kVA, 275 kVA, 300 kVA, 350 kVA. The engineer selects a standard 300 kVA (240 kW) Standby Generator Set, ensuring complete compliance with ISO 8528 and NFPA 110.

Frequently Asked Questions (Generator Sizing)

What happens if a diesel generator is operated at very light loads?

Operating a diesel generator at less than $30\%$ of its rated capacity for extended periods leads to wet stacking. Low combustion cylinder pressures allow unburned fuel and lube oil to leak past piston rings into the exhaust manifold, creating a dark, thick residue. Over time, this leads to heavy carbon deposits on turbocharger vanes, sticking piston rings, and engine power loss. Commercial gensets must be tested annually under full load using resistive load banks.

Can I use a soft starter or VFD to reduce the generator size requirement?

Yes. Installing a solid-state soft starter reduces motor starting inrush from $6.0\times$ running HP down to approximately $3.0\times$, cutting starting kVA in half. A Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) virtually eliminates inrush surge entirely, drawing only $1.0\times$ to $1.2\times$ rated full-load current. By eliminating starting current spikes, you can frequently downsize the generator set by one or two frame sizes.

What is the difference between a natural gas generator and a diesel generator?

Diesel generators provide superior block load acceptance (often capable of absorbing $100\%$ full load in a single step per NFPA 110 10-second start requirements) and store on-site fuel securely. Natural gas generators rely on underground utility pipelines which may be shut off during seismic events or severe storms, and spark-ignited natural gas engines accept lower block load steps ($50\%$ to $70\%$). However, natural gas burns cleaner and has unlimited runtime without refueling logistics.

Why is standard generator power factor rated at 0.8 lagging?

Industrial and commercial power networks consist of inductive components (electric motors, transformers, fluorescent ballasts) which cause alternating current to lag behind voltage. An alternator rated at 100 kVA at 0.8 PF delivers 80 kW of real usable mechanical work while providing 60 kVAR of magnetizing reactive current without exceeding alternator stator thermal limits.
