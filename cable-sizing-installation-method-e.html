Engineering Analysis: Sizing Cables on Cable Trays & Ladders (Method E)

In high-density commercial, institutional, and industrial power distribution, routing cables inside enclosed conduits becomes structurally impractical and cost-prohibitive. Instead, electrical consulting engineers specify overhead cable containment systems utilizing perforated sheet steel cable trays, open cable ladders, or stainless steel wire mesh baskets. Under IEC 60364-5-52 Table A.52.3 and the UK BS 7671 (IET Wiring Regulations, Table 4A2), multicore cables routed in open free air across horizontal or vertical cable trays are codified as Installation Reference Method E.

Method E represents the gold standard for high-amperage sub-main distribution because it maximizes natural aerodynamic ventilation. Convective air currents pass unimpeded through the perforated tray bed (minimum $30\%$ perforation ratio) and open ladder rungs, creating continuous buoyancy drafts that rapidly sweep away joule heat generated by high load currents.

Aerodynamic Convective Boundary Layer Mechanics

The rate of heat transfer from an energized cable sheath into surrounding switchroom air is governed by Newton's Law of Cooling:

$$q_{conv} = h_c \cdot A_{surface} \cdot (T_{sheath} - T_{ambient})$$

Where $h_c$ is the convective heat transfer coefficient. On an open ladder or perforated tray:

  • 360-Degree Perimeter Dissipation: Unlike surface-clipped runs (Method C) where the wall interface impedes airflow across roughly $30\%$ of the sheath, Method E permits complete radial heat convection around the entire $2\pi r$ perimeter.
  • Stack Buoyancy Draft: Warm air heated by bottom cables accelerates upward through perforated tray slots, creating an active natural chimney draft that draws cooler ambient air into the cable bed.
  • Thermal Efficiency Gain: As a result, Method E cables enjoy the highest tabulated continuous ampacities of any standard multi-core installation topology—exceeding enclosed conduit methods (Method A) by up to $60\%$!

Grouping Deratings on Horizontal Cable Trays (Table 4C1)

In high-capacity switchrooms, multiple sub-feeders are routed side-by-side on the same tray. When multi-core cables are bundled touching in a single layer, mutual radiant heating reduces capacity, governed by factor $C_g$:

$$I_z = I_0 \times C_a \times C_g$$

Per IEC 60364-5-52 Table B.52.18 for horizontal perforated trays:

  • 2 circuits touching: $C_g = 0.88$
  • 3 circuits touching: $C_g = 0.82$
  • 4 circuits touching: $C_g = 0.77$
  • 6 circuits touching: $C_g = 0.72$

Spacing Optimization Strategy: If the design engineer maintains a clear spacing of at least one overall cable diameter ($s \ge 1.0 \cdot d_e$) between adjacent cables on the tray, $C_g = 1.0$ (no derating penalty applies). Specifying a wider cable tray (e.g. $450\text{ mm}$ instead of $300\text{ mm}$) frequently avoids thousands of dollars in cable conductor upsizing!

Practical Worked Engineering Example: Industrial MCC Main Sub-Feeder

Design Scenario: Dimensioning a 3-phase, 400V, 50 Hz feeder cable supplying a Motor Control Center (MCC) in a chemical plant. Continuous running load is $I_b = 115\text{ A}$ protected by a $125\text{ A}$ electronic trip MCCB ($I_n = 125\text{ A}$). The cable is routed overhead along a perforated steel cable tray (Method E) for $65\text{ meters}$. The tray carries 2 other parallel multicore feeders (total 3 circuits touching in single layer, $C_g = 0.82$). Plant ambient switchroom temperature peaks at $35^\circ\text{C}$. Multi-core copper cable with XLPE ($90^\circ\text{C}$) insulation is specified. Max allowable voltage drop is $4.0\%$.

Step 1: Compute Environmental Derating ($C_{total}$)

For XLPE at $35^\circ\text{C}$ ambient, $C_a = \sqrt{\frac{90 - 35}{90 - 30}} = 0.957$.

With 3 circuits touching, $C_g = 0.82$.

$$C_{total} = C_a \times C_g = 0.957 \times 0.82 = 0.785$$

Step 2: Minimum Required Tabulated Ampacity ($I_t$)

$$I_{t,req} \ge \frac{I_n}{C_{total}} = \frac{125\text{ A}}{0.785} = 159.24\text{ A}$$

Step 3: Conductor Area Selection from Table 4E2A (Method E XLPE)

  • $25\text{ mm}^2 \implies I_0 = 146\text{ A}$ (Insufficient: $146 < 159.24$)
  • $35\text{ mm}^2 \implies I_0 = 180\text{ A}$ (Compliant: $180 \ge 159.24\text{ A}$)

Effective derated continuous capacity:

$$I_z = 180\text{ A} \times 0.785 = 141.3\text{ A}$$

Since $I_b (115\text{ A}) \le I_n (125\text{ A}) \le I_z (141.3\text{ A})$, overcurrent thermal coordination is fully satisfied.

Step 4: Check Voltage Drop ($\Delta V$) on $35\text{ mm}^2$

For $35\text{ mm}^2$ copper XLPE, the three-phase factor is $1.10\text{ mV/A/m}$:

$$\Delta V = \frac{1.10 \times 115\text{ A} \times 65\text{ m}}{1000} = \frac{8222.5}{1000} = 8.22\text{ Volts}$$

$$\Delta V\% = \frac{8.22\text{ V}}{400\text{ V}} \times 100 = 2.06\%$$

Since $2.06\% \le 4.0\%$, the $35\text{ mm}^2$ XLPE copper cable on tray is fully compliant with all electrical, safety, and operational standards.

Frequently Asked Questions (Installation Method E)

What minimum perforation percentage is required for Method E ratings?

Under IEC 60364-5-52 Table A.52.3, a cable tray must possess perforations occupying at least $30\%$ of the base surface area uniformly distributed along the tray length to qualify for Method E ratings. If a solid-bottom tray without perforations is used, ventilation is restricted and the installation must be derated to Reference Method C.

How do vertical cable ladder runs differ from horizontal trays?

Vertical ladder runs (such as risers in commercial building electrical shafts) benefit from chimney stack thermal convection. However, cables must be clamped with cleats capable of supporting mechanical gravity loads without pinching cable insulation. In IEC 60364-5-52, vertical ladder runs with unobstructed airflow are rated identically to or slightly higher than horizontal trays.

What is the maximum permissible stacking of cables on a cable tray?

Cables should strictly be installed in a single layer whenever possible. If multi-core cables are double-stacked in multiple layers, heat cannot escape from the bottom tier, requiring severe derating factors (typically $C_g \le 0.60$) and risking localized thermal melting. In industrial design, installing a second parallel tray tier is always preferable to stacking.

Do tray covers alter the installation reference method?

Yes. If a solid metal dust or weather cover is installed directly onto a cable tray without ventilation risers, natural convection is blocked and heat is trapped. Standard engineering practice requires derating the cable ampacity by $10\%$ to $15\%$, effectively shifting the reference method from Method E to Method B or Method C.
