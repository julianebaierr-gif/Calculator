Principles of Electrical Resonance in Inductive-Capacitive (LC) Networks

Electrical resonance occurs in alternating-current (AC) circuits containing both inductive ($L$) and capacitive ($C$) energy storage elements when the respective reactive impedances cancel each other out. An inductor stores energy dynamically in its magnetic field proportionally to the square of instantaneous current ($E_L = \frac{1}{2} L I^2$), resisting changes in electric current. A capacitor stores energy electrostatically in its electric field proportionally to the square of voltage ($E_C = \frac{1}{2} C V^2$), resisting changes in potential difference.

When coupled together in an LC resonator (frequently called an LC tank circuit), continuous cyclical energy exchange takes place between the magnetic field of the inductor and the electrostatic field of the capacitor. At the circuit's natural undamped resonant frequency ($f_0$), the total reactive impedance reaches a critical singularity, enabling maximum energy transfer, frequency discrimination, RF filtering, oscillator generation, and wireless communication tuning.

Mathematical Derivation of the LC Resonant Frequency Equation

The complex impedance of an ideal inductor with inductance $L$ (in Henrys) operating at cyclic frequency $f$ and angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ is purely reactive and positive (inductive reactance):

$$Z_L = j X_L = j \omega L = j 2 \pi f L$$

The complex impedance of an ideal capacitor with capacitance $C$ (in Farads) is purely reactive and negative (capacitive reactance):

$$Z_C = -j X_C = \frac{1}{j \omega C} = -j \frac{1}{\omega C} = -j \frac{1}{2 \pi f C}$$

For a series LC network, the total input impedance $Z_{\text{in}}$ across the terminal pair is the arithmetic sum of the branch impedances:

$$Z_{\text{in}}(\omega) = Z_L + Z_C = j \left( \omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C} \right)$$

Resonance is formally defined as the steady-state operating condition where the total reactive component of impedance vanishes to zero ($X_{\text{net}} = 0$):

$$\omega_0 L - \frac{1}{\omega_0 C} = 0 \implies \omega_0 L = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C}$$

Multiplying both sides by $\omega_0 / L$ yields:

$$\omega_0^2 = \frac{1}{L C} \implies \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L C}} \quad \left[ \frac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \right]$$

Converting angular frequency $\omega_0$ to cyclic frequency $f_0$ via $\omega_0 = 2 \pi f_0$ produces Thomson's classical resonance formula:

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2 \pi \sqrt{L C}} \quad [\text{Hz}]$$

By algebraic rearrangement, if a target resonant frequency $f_0$ is mandated by RF spectrum regulations or IF passband requirements, the necessary reactive components are computed directly:

$$L = \frac{1}{4 \pi^2 f_0^2 C}, \qquad C = \frac{1}{4 \pi^2 f_0^2 L}$$

Series vs Parallel Resonant Networks: Dual Topologies and Impedance Curves

While the mathematical resonance condition $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ is identical for both configurations, series and parallel LC topologies present completely inverted impedance characteristics to external signal sources:

Quality Factor ($Q$), Damping Ratio, and Half-Power Bandwidth ($\Delta f$)

Real-world inductors and capacitors are never completely lossless. Real inductors contain copper wire DC resistance and high-frequency skin effect losses ($R_s$), while dielectric dissipation manifests as parallel leakage. The Quality Factor ($Q$) characterizes how underdamped the resonant oscillation is, defined as $2\pi$ times the ratio of maximum energy stored to energy dissipated per cycle:

$$Q = 2 \pi \left( \frac{\text{Maximum Energy Stored}}{\text{Energy Dissipated Per Cycle}} \right)$$

For a series RLC network with series loss resistance $R$:

$$Q_{\text{series}} = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C R} = \frac{1}{R} \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \frac{Z_0}{R}$$

Where $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}$ is the characteristic tank impedance. The half-power ($-3\text{ dB}$) bandwidth $\Delta f = f_2 - f_1$ between upper and lower frequencies where terminal power drops by $50\%$ ($1/\sqrt{2}$ in voltage) is tightly coupled to $Q$:

$$\Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q}$$

Step-by-Step Engineering Case Study: FM Radio Intermediate Frequency (IF) Filter

Scenario: An RF broadcast receiver requires an intermediate frequency (IF) bandpass filter centered at standard $f_0 = 10.7 \ \text{MHz}$ with a $-3 \ \text{dB}$ channel selectivity bandwidth of $\Delta f = 200 \ \text{kHz}$. A high-frequency precision inductor of $L = 2.2 \ \mu\text{H}$ is selected.

Step 1: Calculate Required Tuning Capacitance ($C$):

$$C = \frac{1}{4 \pi^2 f_0^2 L} = \frac{1}{4 \pi^2 \times (10.7 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz})^2 \times (2.2 \times 10^{-6} \text{ H})}$$ $$C = \frac{1}{39.4784 \times 1.1449 \times 10^{14} \times 2.2 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{1}{9.944 \times 10^{9}} = 1.0056 \times 10^{-10} \text{ F} = 100.56 \text{ pF}$$

Step 2: Determine Required Quality Factor ($Q$):

$$Q = \frac{f_0}{\Delta f} = \frac{10.7 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz}}{200 \times 10^3 \text{ Hz}} = 53.5$$

Step 3: Calculate Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$):

$$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.2 \times 10^{-6}}{100.56 \times 10^{-12}}} = \sqrt{21,877.5} = 147.91 \ \Omega$$

Step 4: Compute Maximum Allowable Total Series Damping Resistance ($R_{\text{max}}$):

$$R = \frac{Z_0}{Q} = \frac{147.91 \ \Omega}{53.5} = 2.76 \ \Omega$$

Conclusion: To achieve the exact $10.7 \text{ MHz}$ center frequency and $200 \text{ kHz}$ broadcast bandwidth, the circuit must utilize a $100.6 \text{ pF}$ low-loss C0G dielectric capacitor and ensure total inductor copper plus PCB trace resistance does not exceed $2.76 \ \Omega$.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What happens physically at LC electrical resonance?

At electrical resonance, the inductive reactance $X_L = 2\pi f L$ and capacitive reactance $X_C = 1/(2\pi f C)$ become equal in magnitude and $180^\circ$ out of phase, canceling each other completely. In a series LC circuit, net impedance drops to purely resistive $R$ (minimum impedance), allowing maximum current. In a parallel LC tank, net impedance surges to a maximum, presenting an open-circuit barrier to the resonant frequency.

How do you calculate the resonant frequency of an LC circuit?

The formula is derived by equating inductive and capacitive reactance: $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$, leading to angular frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and cyclic frequency $f_0 = \frac{1}{2 \pi \sqrt{LC}}$, where $L$ is inductance in Henrys and $C$ is capacitance in Farads.

What is the Quality Factor (Q) of a resonant circuit?

Quality factor $Q$ measures damping and selectivity. It is defined as $2\pi$ times the ratio of peak energy stored to energy dissipated per cycle. For a series RLC circuit, $Q = \frac{1}{R} \sqrt{L/C}$. For a parallel RLC circuit, $Q = R \sqrt{C/L}$. Higher $Q$ results in a narrower $3\text{ dB}$ bandwidth.

How is 3 dB bandwidth related to resonant frequency and Q?

The half-power ($-3\text{ dB}$) bandwidth $\Delta f$ is inversely proportional to $Q$: $\Delta f = f_0 / Q$. High-$Q$ circuits exhibit sharp selectivity with narrow bandwidth, while low-$Q$ circuits have broader passbands.
